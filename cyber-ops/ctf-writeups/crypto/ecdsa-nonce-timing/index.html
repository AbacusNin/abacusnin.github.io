Six Fast Signatures and a Private Key

I had 1,600 signatures, but six short signing times carried the key.

Summary

The challenge. Recover a P-256 signing key from signature timing, then unwrap a short encrypted payload.

What I did. I fitted a nonce bit-length timing model, reduced a six-signature Hidden Number Problem lattice, verified the scalar against the public key, and tried a bounded keystream matrix.

The result. The replay recovered a scalar whose P-256 public point exactly matches the supplied point. The matching ciphertext yields 35 printable bytes in flag format under the recorded hash-chain method.

The limit. The scalar and plaintext are withheld. The available record has no platform acceptance receipt and does not identify a real vulnerable product or signer.

1. Problem Statement and Threat Scenario

The challenge gave me a CSV of 1,600 P-256 ECDSA signatures. Each row had a message digest h, signature values r and s, and an elapsed signing time in nanoseconds. A second file gave the public point Q = (Qx, Qy). The last piece was a 35-byte encrypted payload. My job was to recover the long-term signing scalar d and use it to open that payload.

The signer itself was not present. I had observations of its work, not an API I could call. The threat model is a service or device that lets an observer pair signatures with signing duration. If scalar multiplication runs for a number of iterations determined by the secret nonce's bit length, time reveals a bound on that nonce. Several such bounds can expose the long-term key. The immediate damage is loss of the signing identity; forged signatures become possible. In this puzzle, the same scalar also keyed a payload wrapper.

I could verify the cryptographic claim offline. A recovered d either produces the supplied public point under P-256 or it does not. I kept that check separate from the later plaintext search. I have no acceptance screen or live signer log, so the page claims a locally verified recovery and a flag-shaped payload, not a recorded platform award.

2. Techniques, Tooling, and Actions Taken

I started with the boring file checks: three artifacts, a 1,600-row CSV, and well-formed hex in the signature columns. The timer values did not look like one smooth cloud. Most were around 195,000 to 198,000 ns. Six were much lower, from 139,661 to 163,860 ns. Those six made more sense as short nonces than as random scheduling noise once I checked their spacing against a simple timing model.

I tried more than 30 key, mode, and nonce combinations. Raw scalar XOR gave only 8 printable bytes out of 35. AES-ECB and CBC did not fit a 35-byte bare ciphertext under ordinary block framing; CTR and ChaCha20 branches did not yield the expected prefix. Those failures matter because recovering d did not automatically tell me how the puzzle wrapped its payload. I used a finite probe matrix instead of staring at the ciphertext and guessing.

I also considered brute-forcing the 24-bit nonce from ID 1254. That would be a useful check, but it would solve only the easiest row and teach little about traces with weaker leakage. I stayed with the lattice path. My own replay used a bounded, pure-Python LLL implementation and recovered a scalar that passes the public-key check. It also identified the hash-chain branch without printing the scalar or plaintext. The replay script lets the checks be repeated with locally held challenge files.

3. Tooling and Scripts

Timing-leak triage

The first useful calculation was the timing fit. Taking 197,662 ns as the full-width anchor gives 197662 - 256 * 250 = 133662 ns. The smallest measurement, 139,661 ns, lands at (139661 - 133662) / 250 = 23.996 bits. The other five low rows land near 62, 62, 79, 79, and 121. That pattern is specific enough to justify trying a nonce-width model; an isolated low outlier would not be.

My broad step-search script is a heuristic. It asks how many elapsed values fall near integer multiples of candidate steps; it does not by itself prove the signer leaks nonce length. The subsequent public-key recovery is the much stronger test. For defensive validation, the controlled latency checker takes a test-run CSV with known nonce_bits and elapsed_ns, rather than pretending a production log already knows each nonce width.

HNP lattice primitive (reusable)

From each ECDSA signature, I can compute alpha and beta without the private key. With a bound on k, the value alpha * d + beta mod n must be small after centering. Six such rows define a short-vector search. The local replay builds the eight-row integer basis, performs LLL with bounded work, extracts candidate scalars, and accepts one only when P-256 multiplication reproduces Q. Its pure-Python reducer is transparent for this case; a noisier or larger HNP instance deserves a dedicated lattice library and more careful per-row bounds.

Driver: trace to private key

The replay script accepts the CSV and a text file containing Qx and Qy. It selects the six shortest times, applies the case's 133662 ns intercept, 250 ns-per-bit slope, and four-bit safety pad, then checks the recovered scalar. On the matching local files it reports all six selected IDs, bit bounds [28, 66, 66, 83, 83, 125], and public_key_match True. It deliberately prints no scalar. Those numbers explain the solve without turning the page into a key disclosure.

Payload unwrap: keystream probe matrix

The same replay script tries thirteen local branches: repeating raw-scalar XOR, a SHA-256 counter stream under three key encodings, AES-CTR with a zero counter, and ChaCha20 with eight- and twelve-byte zero nonces. It reports the best branch and output shape, never the plaintext. Against the 35-byte ASCII-hex ciphertext, hash-chain XOR / SHA256(scalar bytes) is the branch with 35 printable bytes and a closing brace. I also tried ECB/CBC variants, which I rejected before decryption because the bare ciphertext length was not a block multiple. A different envelope could contain other framing, so length alone is a clue rather than a cipher proof.

4. Concept Explanation

ECDSA in one paragraph

An ECDSA signer keeps a long-term scalar d and publishes Q = d * G. For each signature it uses a fresh nonce k, computes a point from k * G, and returns (r, s) where s = k^-1(h + r*d) mod n. If one complete k becomes known, the equation gives d immediately. A few partial nonce bounds can be enough too. FIPS 186-5 defines the signature algorithm; the implementation still has to protect the nonce while computing it.

The Hidden Number Problem

Rearranging the signature equation gives k_i = beta_i + alpha_i*d mod n, with alpha_i = s_i^-1*r_i mod n and beta_i = s_i^-1*h_i mod n. I know the values on the right except for d. The timing model tells me that each selected k_i sits below a particular power of two. I encode those bounds in a lattice so a vector associated with the real d is unusually short. LLL finds candidates; the public point checks the answer. The bounds and scaling are implementation choices, so I do not present an arbitrary short vector as a recovered key.

Why timing leaks bit length

If scalar multiplication loops once per significant bit of k, a 24-bit nonce takes far fewer iterations than a 256-bit one. Here the observed times fit about 250 ns per bit. That is a Minerva-class failure mode, not proof that a particular library named in the research was used in this puzzle. The Minerva project documents real implementations and noisy-bit-length lattice attacks. A deterministic nonce generator under RFC 6979 can avoid random-generator failures, but it still feeds a variable-width scalar to multiplication. Constant-time behavior must be checked in that arithmetic path too.

5. Exploit/Analysis Mechanics

The violated invariant

The signature must not reveal useful information about k. Here duration was a side channel into its bit length. ECDSA verification still worked normally, which is why correctness tests alone would miss the defect. The weakness was in how the signature was computed, not in the public verification equation.

The math

The six chosen rows give s_i*k_i = h_i + r_i*d (mod n). After multiplying by s_i^-1, I get k_i = alpha_i*d + beta_i (mod n). I padded each estimated bit length by four to avoid rejecting a true nonce because of a noisy timer. The bounds used for recovery were 28, 66, 66, 83, 83, and 125 bits. Their uneven strength matters: the shortest nonce gives far more information than a near-full-width one.

The centered basis used a common width W = 2^125, diagonal terms n^2, a row of n*alpha_i, and a row of n*(beta_i - W/2). The public-key equality is the final check. The exact lattice basis is in the linked code; it would be wrong to say that any set of six timing outliers solves P-256. This trace's bounds are unusually strong.

Recovery and validation (ST.6 through ST.7)

My first verification attempt failed because the hand-written point-addition code tried to invert a zero denominator when it encountered inverse points. That exception did not refute the lattice candidate. I switched to a maintained P-256 implementation and compared d * G with the supplied Qx and Qy. The independent replay passes. The script now makes that equality the acceptance gate and keeps the scalar off standard output.

Why decryption was a small search (ST.8-9)

The payload had 35 bytes after decoding its hex text. I tested a short list of stream-shaped wrappers and key derivations. The successful branch was SHA-256 of the 32-byte scalar, followed by SHA-256 blocks over that key and a four-byte big-endian counter, XORed with the ciphertext. It produced 35 printable bytes in flag format. That is a local cryptographic result. Without a platform receipt, I leave platform acceptance unstated.

6. Lessons Learned (Practitioner Perspective)

What the exercise validated

The timing histogram earned its place before the algebra. Those six values were low enough to be interesting, and the near-integer spacing gave me a testable cost model. The key check against Q settled whether the lattice had found something real. A pretty plaintext was useful later, but the public key was the stronger oracle for the private scalar.

What the exercise surfaced as gaps

The hand-written curve helper wasted time on an edge case a maintained implementation already handles. I kept the failing path in the record because it explains why the verifier changed. I also underestimated the payload wrapper work after recovering d. A prepared matrix of plausible key encodings and stream modes was faster than trying them serially by hunch.

Transferable principles

For a signer review, measure the implementation under controlled conditions and keep timing collection separate from signing secrets. For an investigation, validate each hypothesis against an independent artifact. In this case that was the public key. In a production incident it might be a certificate, an authenticated transaction, or the exact signer build. Do not promote a timing correlation into proof of exploitation without that second check.

7. Extrapolation

Individual practitioner skill development

This case made the Hidden Number Problem concrete for me. The portable skills are choosing useful signatures, turning timing into defensible bounds, setting up a lattice, and rejecting candidate keys unless they match the public point. Those skills carry to other biased-nonce ECDSA cases. RSA partial-key work and hardware power analysis are adjacent subjects, but this page does not claim to have exercised either one.

Security operations (SOC, IR, threat hunting)

A defender should first inventory signers and their cryptographic libraries, then run a controlled side-channel test against affected versions. The nonce-width latency checker accepts a development-build benchmark where the nonce width is known to the tester. Its leaky, flat, and insufficient-data fixtures make the expected behavior visible. The test suite checks all three cases. A flat result from one run cannot certify constant-time behavior; the test plan must account for noise, hardware, compiler, and input distribution.

An operational hunt can review unusually high volumes of signing requests by one client or key. That may help find an active collection campaign, but the puzzle's trace does not contain client identity or request provenance, and passive observation might produce no extra requests at all. If a key's nonce exposure is credibly established, the response is to contain the signer, replace the affected implementation, assess signature trust, rotate the key, and revoke dependent certificates as appropriate. The order and urgency depend on the service's actual trust role.

Intelligence operations

An intelligence record should identify the affected signer implementation and observation channel, not merely label every ECDSA timing difference "Minerva." The research describes a technique class; this challenge does not identify a campaign, actor, library version, or exploited host. The illustrative STIX bundle contains an attack pattern and a mitigation relationship without inventing those missing facts. Historical signatures alone do not reveal signing times; a bias claim needs the relevant observation or another measurable nonce weakness.

8. Reusable Tools, Scripts, and Artifacts

Triage decision tree

I use this order: confirm that timing is tied to individual signatures; establish the normal timing spread; look for a repeatable relation to known nonce widths in a controlled build; estimate bounds conservatively; then attempt key recovery and public-key validation. A statistical outlier count is a lead. A public-key match is proof that the candidate scalar is correct for that key. If the timing model fails, I stop the lattice work and check other explanations before collecting more rows.

Generic HNP primitive

The HNP replay contains the arithmetic, basis construction, bounded LLL reducer, public-point check, and payload probe. It is a transparent implementation of this six-row case. Its common W and case-specific timing slope should be reviewed before applying it to other curves or noisier traces. A production cryptography assessment should use a maintained lattice package and independent key checks.

Solve-pattern checklist

The lab guide gives the runnable commands and the evidence boundaries. In brief: count and validate rows, fit a falsifiable leak model, add safety margins to nonce bounds, compute alpha and beta, reduce the basis, verify the public key, then test a small payload matrix. Record rejected modes and the exact validation outcome. Keep the private scalar and plaintext out of logs and committed artifacts.

Payload-unwrap probe matrix template

The matrix in the replay script tries thirteen stream-shaped candidates and reports the best branch without displaying plaintext. It includes raw-scalar XOR, hash-chain XOR, AES-CTR, and two ChaCha20 nonce lengths under three key encodings. I have not called that list exhaustive. A production wrapper should be identified from its specification and authenticated metadata, not inferred from printability.

10. Threat Intel and Standards Integration

STIX 2.1 sample objects

The technique bundle contains a generic attack-pattern, a course-of-action, and a mitigates relationship. It does not contain an indicator with a fake hash, IP address, or version string. I checked that its identifiers are UUIDv4-shaped and its references resolve inside the bundle; a full STIX schema implementation and TAXII exchange were not run. A real sharing package would need an affected product/version, observation channel, evidence, confidence, producer identity, and handling rules. OASIS STIX 2.1 defines those object types.

STIGs

No specific DISA application STIG finding can be concluded from an anonymous signer trace. A system owner could review its approved cryptographic module and maintenance baseline once the product and environment are known. FIPS validation addresses a module's validated boundary and configuration; it does not, by itself, establish that this exact timing path is constant time.

CIS Controls v8

Control 16's vetted application-security components and Control 7's vulnerability remediation are useful program-level homes for the fix. They point to inventorying the signer, evaluating vendor evidence, testing the affected code path, and patching or replacing it. Encrypt-in-transit and encrypt-at-rest safeguards are too broad to explain this particular nonce leak.

These are design mappings for a hypothetical deployment. The puzzle did not establish a compliance boundary, a vulnerable package version, or an actual key-rotation decision.

11b. Mitigation realization

The first engineering fix is a signing implementation that does not let secret nonce width change the observable execution path. Test the real compiler, library build, hardware, and signing API. The controlled benchmark reviewer gives a small positive and negative demonstration, while the guide spells out why its threshold is not a certification. An HSM can help only when the specific module and path resist this side channel. RFC 6979 helps prevent weak or repeated nonce generation; it leaves variable-time scalar multiplication to be fixed separately.

For response planning, identify every identity that used the affected implementation, then decide whether to rotate and revoke. A generic policy file that bans guessed version ranges would be dangerous here because no product build was identified. I would derive that policy from vendor advisories and an SBOM, test it in CI, and retain the mapping from a finding to the exact deployed binary.

12. Indicators, Artifacts, and Detection Engineering

Detection maturity here is controlled-lab screening. The key recovery was verified offline. The defensive benchmark was tested only with invented values; it has not been run against a signing service, SIEM, or production baseline.

Sanitized illustrative IOCs

There are no atomic IOCs in this challenge. The Qx/Qy point and signature rows are puzzle inputs, not a malicious infrastructure indicator. A proposed hunt might look for a client generating many distinct signing requests in a short period. That behavior has legitimate causes, and a passive observer could avoid it entirely. I would not share a placeholder library hash or <signing.host>:443 as intelligence.

Sigma rule

My first Sigma sketch tried to combine a percentile threshold and a per-source count inside one rule's ordinary selection. That does not express a portable correlation. I have not published it as a working detector. A real rule needs a product log schema, a trusted baseline per key and host, a time-windowed count, and backend conversion support. The Sigma correlation specification is the starting point for that implementation.

YARA rule (library version fingerprint)

The proposed YARA expression relied on a placeholder version string and symbols that may be stripped. It would miss many affected builds and could match unrelated ELF files. I would use an authenticated SBOM, package metadata, and vendor-fixed version ranges for inventory. YARA can be a triage aid after validating a real binary signature against positive and negative samples; this case supplies neither.

SIEM queries

These are high-volume signing-request hunts, not detectors for private-key recovery. They require client_id, key_id, and a digest identifier from an application audit stream. The threshold of 1,000 requests in an hour is illustrative and needs a service baseline.

index=signing_audit event_type=ecdsa_sign earliest=-1h
| bin _time span=1h
| stats count as sign_count dc(message_digest) as distinct_digests by _time key_id client_id
| where sign_count>=1000 AND distinct_digests>=1000

SigningAudit
| where TimeGenerated > ago(1h) and EventType == "ecdsa_sign"
| summarize SignCount=count(), DistinctDigests=dcount(MessageDigest)
    by ClientId, KeyId, bin(TimeGenerated, 1h)
| where SignCount >= 1000 and DistinctDigests >= 1000

Log source and pipeline requirements

For request-volume hunting, the signer needs a stable key ID, caller ID, request time, and a safe digest identifier. Avoid putting message contents, nonce values, private-key handles, or plaintext in general-purpose logs. For the controlled timing test, a separate development harness must know each test nonce width and record a high-resolution elapsed time. Most production logs cannot supply that ground truth, and network TLS handshake duration includes many steps besides ECDSA signing. The invented fixtures and flat comparison show what the isolated measurement looks like.

Detection engineering notes

The lab tests pass a leaky fixture, a flat fixture, an insufficient-data rejection, and the STIX bundle's internal-reference check. The sparse fixture is linked so the rejection can be inspected. The synthetic 250 ns-per-bit signal is deliberately easy to see; real noise may erase it. No false-positive rate, backend query result, or product-level constant-time claim has been measured. The most reliable response to a confirmed timing leak is to remove the leak and assess affected keys. A SIEM outlier alone does not tell me whether a nonce was short or an observer recovered anything.
