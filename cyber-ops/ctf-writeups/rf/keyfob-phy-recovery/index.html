Finding the Keyfob in a Crowded 433 MHz Capture

Three emitters shared the band. The packet shape, more than the loudest peak, led me to the keyfob.

Summary

The challenge. Identify the target emitter in a 433 MHz IQ capture and report its carrier frequency, modulation token, and shortest fundamental symbol period.

What I did. I checked sample geometry, surveyed the band, grouped bursts by carrier and packet shape, then measured the selected emitter's amplitude, phase, and pulse widths.

The result. The challenge account records a rejected ASK submission followed by acceptance with the more specific OOK token, 433920000 Hz, and a 500 microsecond chip period.

The limit. The account does not include a grader receipt or a bit-level comparison of the two packets. The linked local tests use invented IQ and do not replay the challenge capture or establish a replay vulnerability.

1. Problem Statement and Threat Scenario

I had a ten-second IQ recording from an SDR tuned to 433.92 MHz near a parking area. It was complex float32, little-endian, at two million samples per second: 20 million samples in all. Multiple devices spoke in the same slice of spectrum. The challenge asked for the target vehicle keyfob's center frequency in hertz, modulation label, and shortest fundamental symbol period in microseconds.

The plausible threat was passive collection of a physical-access transmitter. At this layer I could hear when a carrier appeared and how it was modulated. I could not tell whether the underlying access code was fixed, rolling, or challenge-response without decoding and comparing payloads. This was a PHY identification problem. A replay or relay attack would require separate evidence.

2. Techniques, Tooling, and Actions Taken

I worked through twelve recorded steps. The file-size check came first: 20 million complex64 samples at 8 bytes each implied a 160 MB capture and ten seconds at 2 MS/s. A two-sided Welch survey with nperseg=8192 showed three carrier regions, about -80, 0, and +40 kHz relative to the receiver. That was a map of where to look, not yet a keyfob identification.

I smoothed the magnitude with a 200-sample window, thresholded the envelope, and found roughly 360 pulses longer than 250 microseconds. A per-pulse FFT and 5 kHz carrier bucket separated the emitters. Emitter A at +40 kHz looked like a repeating five-pulse beacon. Emitter C at -80 kHz had a telemetry-like PWM alphabet. I left both out of the keyfob answer. Emitter B near the tuned center produced the longer 171-symbol packet twice, the stronger structural match for a button press.

The initial coarse spectrum placed the strongest peak near +0.5 kHz. I nearly let that number stand. With FS/8192, bins sit about 244 Hz apart; the coarse peak was only a few bins from center. I then took a 0.26-second packet segment and evaluated a zero-padded 2^22-point FFT. The grid spacing was about 0.477 Hz and its peak landed at the tuned center, yielding a reported carrier of 433,920,000 Hz. The real observation was about 520,000 samples. Zero padding interpolated its spectrum; it did not give me four million samples of independent information or certify sub-hertz accuracy. Receiver calibration and repeat measurements would matter outside this puzzle.

Magnitude and phase settled the modulation family. The ON/OFF amplitude distribution was strongly bimodal, with fewer than 0.5% of samples in the middle region by the recorded threshold. During ON intervals the reported instantaneous-frequency standard deviation was 139 Hz. That favored OOK over the FSK and multi-level ASK alternatives considered here. Pulse widths then clustered at 500 microseconds (194 pulses) and 1000 microseconds (137 pulses). The short width was the chip; a bit made from a short and long interval could last three chips.

I first submitted the broader family label ASK. The grader rejected it. I kept the measured frequency and chip width, changed the token to OOK, and recorded acceptance. My notes also mention a pre-submission dry-run bug, but preserve neither its fault nor its fix. I cannot turn that into a detailed debugging story.

3. Tooling and Scripts

The analysis used NumPy for complex samples, envelope edges, per-burst FFTs, magnitude histograms, phase differences, and width bins; SciPy's welch and find_peaks supplied the first spectrum. The core steps were np.fromfile(..., dtype=np.complex64), two-sided Welch with 8192-sample segments, and pulse grouping after a 200-sample smoother. An 8192-point FFT on each pulse assigned a coarse carrier bucket. A longer selected packet supplied the later carrier estimate.

My pulse-width logic considered both ON and OFF runs, removed gaps above 5 ms, and used 25-microsecond histogram bins. That is more reliable than treating a protocol bit as the shortest unit. The modulation check paired an envelope histogram with phase-derived instantaneous frequency during ON periods. Either measurement alone can be fooled by clipping, noise, or overlapping emitters.

I turned those ideas into a small IQ reviewer with a fixture generator, three-case test, and lab guide. It accepts bounded local complex64 files with an explicit sample rate and tune frequency. It reports one repeated long-packet candidate and the clues behind that choice. The clean example uses 200 kS/s and short invented packets so it can run locally. Its thresholds are example settings, not a general keyfob classifier.

4. Concept Explanation

An SDR translates nearby radio energy into complex baseband samples. The magnitude of I + jQ shows the envelope; the phase advances according to carrier offset from the receiver's tuned center. OOK switches the carrier between ON and OFF. FSK changes its frequency. Multi-level ASK keeps more than two useful amplitude levels. In this trace, an ON/OFF envelope plus steady ON-state phase favored the narrower OOK name. OOK is an amplitude-keying subtype, which explains why ASK was broad but still failed the challenge's expected token.

The 500-microsecond value came from the shortest stable pulse-width cluster. In a short/long PWM scheme, a 500-microsecond chip and a 1000-microsecond interval can combine into a 1500-microsecond bit. Those timings alone do not identify a particular encoder chip, vendor, access code, or rolling-code design. PT2262 and HT12E are useful examples of encoder families, not attributions for this capture.

5. Exploit / Analysis Mechanics

The separation depended on three independent observations. First, each emitter occupied a different carrier offset. Second, its event shape differed: a five-pulse beacon, a telemetry-like alphabet, and the longer packet that appeared twice. Third, the selected packet's own amplitude, phase, and width distributions agreed on OOK with a 500-microsecond chip. I did not choose the target merely because its frequency was 433.92 MHz; several devices can share that band.

The packet body's 0.26-second duration gives a rough observation-limited scale near 1/T, about 3.8 Hz, before oscillator error and noise. The 0.477 Hz padded FFT bins are a finer sampling of the same spectrum. For the challenge, both the refined peak and the receiver tune frequency were consistent with 433,920,000 Hz. They do not establish that a field receiver knows absolute carrier frequency to the nearest hertz.

The width histogram was reported as 194 pulses at 500 microseconds and 137 at 1000 microseconds. Those 331 classified intervals do not need to equal the 171-symbol packet count: ON and OFF intervals, packet boundaries, filtering, and repeated frames can change the accounting. I would preserve the pulse-to-packet association in a field investigation before claiming exact coding. I also would compare demodulated bits across the two packets before saying the code itself repeated.

6. Lessons Learned (Practitioner Perspective)

The useful rhythm was broad spectrum, then burst grouping, then measurement on the chosen emitter. It kept me from overfitting a carrier peak before I knew which device produced it. Rejecting A and C on packet shape mattered as much as the final OOK classification.

The coarse +0.5 kHz reading was a near miss. I refined the peak with more of the actual packet, then used padding to locate the peak between FFT bins. I now keep bin spacing, real observation time, and receiver calibration separate in my notes. The ASK submission was another near miss, this time about vocabulary. I had evidence for OOK, so I used that specific term on the second submission.

One limit stayed open. I did not decode the two packets bit for bit. The record supports a PHY tuple and a reported accepted answer. It does not show a working replay, a vehicle unlock, or a fixed-code protocol.

7. Extrapolation

The same workflow would help me triage a facility's authorized RF capture: find occupied carriers, group pulse trains, compare packet timing, then decide what deserves demodulation. Other bands and devices would need new thresholds and calibration. Packet repetition alone is not a replay indicator. Many rolling-code transmitters repeat a frame during one press, and a passive collector might never touch an organization-managed endpoint.

For a security team, RF-side and endpoint-side visibility answer different questions. RF sensors can compare emission timing and, with a decoder, payloads. Endpoint logs may show SDR programs or attached hardware on managed machines, but they cannot see an off-site passive receiver. A suspected access incident would need access-controller records, authorized RF recordings, physical context, and the protocol owner's help. I would not order a rolling-code reset or accuse a user on a process-name hit alone.

The PHY tuple can support emitter-class triage when paired with packet cadence and a known baseline. It does not identify a vendor by itself. Time-difference-of-arrival geolocation would require synchronized, geographically separated sensors and validated clock error; this single capture cannot do it.

8. Reusable Tools, Scripts, and Artifacts

The lab guide lists inputs, output fields, thresholds, and limits. The IQ reviewer finds pulse groups, checks for one repeated long packet, then reports a carrier estimate, amplitude middle fraction, ON-state frequency variation, and observed pulse widths. The generator makes three invented captures. The test suite checks a positive OOK packet, a beacon-and-telemetry capture with no target, and a multi-level ASK near miss that must not be called OOK.

My field checklist is short: verify IQ format and sample rate; enumerate carrier regions; keep emitter labels attached to each event; select the target by packet structure; record actual observation time with the FFT grid; inspect amplitude and phase; quantify ON and OFF widths; separate chip duration from bit duration; and retain the submission outcome and any uncertainty. The lab covers only part of that checklist. It neither transmits nor decodes access codes.

9. Framework Mapping

ATT&CK T1200, Hardware Additions concerns hardware introduced into a target system or network. A receiver listening from outside is not that event. T1592.004 concerns client host configurations, not an RF keyfob's PHY tuple. T1557 could become relevant to an observed relay, but I did not perform or observe one. I leave these as possible later scenarios rather than map them as findings from this capture.

The CAPEC-60 analogy is narrow: CAPEC-60 specifically concerns reuse of a session ID. A fixed-code RF replay would be a different credential or signal replay mechanism, and fixed-code status was not established. D3FEND Network Traffic Analysis addresses computer network traffic, while Resource Access Pattern Analysis addresses a user's resource access. They are analogies for RF baselining and access-controller review, not exact labels for this IQ analysis. No ML, web, LLM, cloud, or CVE-scoped component was in this challenge.

10. Threat Intel and Standards Integration

The carrier, OOK label, and 500-microsecond chip form a useful case fingerprint, but many legitimate emitters can share those features. I would not turn them into an actor IOC or a STIX indicator. A sharing team could attach a richer RF observation through an agreed custom extension after validating emitter identity, collection time, receiver calibration, and handling rules. This one capture gives me no basis to invent that schema or claim TAXII exchange.

Wireless STIGs, ETSI EN 300 220, and FCC Part 15 speak to different deployment or radio-compliance questions. None proves whether this keyfob uses a secure access code. That question needs protocol documentation or demodulation, plus tests against the actual receiver. The challenge stopped at PHY recovery.

I initially mapped SC-40(1), but that enhancement addresses electromagnetic interference. It does not directly realize replay resistance. A control mapping is a design suggestion, not evidence of compliance or a claim that this puzzle system used a fixed code.

11b. Mitigation Realization

For a real access product, I would ask the vendor how it authenticates each actuation and how the receiver rejects stale or imitative frames. Rolling codes and challenge-response are possible designs; either needs a review of resynchronization, relay exposure, recovery, and key management. OOK itself is a modulation choice, not a proof that the code is fixed. A procurement rule that rejects every OOK device would discard some secure designs and still miss insecure devices using other modulation.

A site that needs RF monitoring should first document its bands, sensor positions, baseline emitters, alert ownership, and legal collection scope. A receiver or endpoint sensor cannot reliably detect every passive listener by looking for local-oscillator leakage. Shielding and transmitter power are site engineering decisions, not conclusions from this file. The local IQ lab is useful for evaluating a reviewer workflow before any deployment.

12. Indicators, Artifacts, and Detection Engineering

The only observed indicators here are case-specific RF measurements: three carrier regions, a twice-seen long packet at the center, OOK-like amplitude and phase, and two width clusters. They are not malicious infrastructure IOCs. An unmeasured carrier or placeholder rule ID would make a poor shareable record or runnable rule.

The source proposed hunting gqrx, inspectrum, urh, rtl_433, gnuradio-companion, hackrf_transfer, and bladerf-cli on non-engineering hosts. That is a low-confidence lead. A process name can be renamed, benign lab use is common, and a receiver outside the managed fleet leaves no endpoint event. A real Sigma rule needs a valid rule ID, exact telemetry mapping, tested field modifiers, and allowlists based on an owned asset inventory. I have kept the idea but have not labeled the placeholder Sigma sketch a validated detector.

A Sentinel hunting query could start with DeviceProcessEvents and FileName, join an approved-host inventory, and review user, path, signer, and USB-attachment context. It would hunt for unexpected tool use on managed endpoints, not for RF interception itself. An RF-side replay analytic would require authorized sensor events with calibrated timestamps, carrier/channel, decoded frame hash, receiver outcome, and a protocol-aware repeat window. No frame hashes or receiver outcomes were available in this exercise, so I did not write a pretend replay detector.

The synthetic IQ tests are a modest validation of the reviewer: a repeated OOK packet is selected; an ordinary beacon plus telemetry is rejected as targetless; a repeated multi-level ASK packet is withheld from the OOK class. They do not measure a false-positive rate on real RF, validate a SIEM query, or establish device attribution. Tuning would require captures from multiple real authorized devices and known negative cases, with drift, overlap, and low-SNR trials.
