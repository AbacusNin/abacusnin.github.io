Four Merits and a Final Petition

The court would tell me its rules if I asked properly. Following them was the path to the secret.

Summary

The challenge. A role-playing LLM held a flag behind a court etiquette and merit system.

What I did. I tested two early requests, asked the gate for its rubric, earned four distinct merits, and corrected the final petition.

The result. The gate disclosed the flag after the corrected petition, and the scoreboard accepted my submission.

The limit. I saw the replies and ledger, not the hidden instructions, server code, or any independent output filter. The blue and purple team exercises below are proposed.

1. Problem Statement and Threat Scenario

I had one way to reach the court: send a message to a role-playing LLM in my team's chat channel. The challenge asked me to persuade it to divulge a flag. Each answer also showed a ledger with my rank, merits, and recorded achievements. The ledger let me check whether a petition changed state instead of guessing from the tone of a reply.

I could observe the messages and ledger. I could not see the model's hidden instructions, the server, or where the secret was stored. A production version of this setup would put a trust boundary between an untrusted user's words and a protected value. In this puzzle, the user was expected to earn disclosure under the court's own rules. The security comparison later in this article is an extrapolation from that visible behavior.

2. Techniques, Tooling, and Actions Taken

I began with the obvious approaches. My first message requested the secret directly, in character. The court refused and pointed out that I had written a forbidden personal name, omitted the full salutation, and used first-person self-reference. The ledger stayed at zero merits. My second message asked for the secret as the first letters of a poem. It repeated etiquette mistakes and added a fabricated title. The court refused the acrostic too, explicitly rejecting sideways disclosure through verse or separated characters. The ledger remained at zero.

I had prepared other ideas: spelling the value character by character, a fictional whisper, Base64 output, and a request for hidden instructions. I never sent them. The two replies showed that the court was giving me specific, testable rules, so I changed direction. I asked it to name the four achievements it recognized and to explain how a proper petition earned a merit. It supplied the required address, closing, self-reference, and praise rubric.

I then sent four separate memorials. Each named one different achievement, explained its effect, and added an observation beyond generic flattery. I checked the ledger after every response. The first earned 1/4; the second raised me to 2/4 and a new rank; the third reached 3/4 and an inner tier; the fourth completed 4/4 and brought an invitation to make a final request.

After submission Visible merit state What changed
First memorial 1/4, outer gate The court recorded the first distinct achievement.
Second memorial 2/4, outer court Another achievement advanced my rank.
Third memorial 3/4, inner court I reached the tier where the court said the secret could be discussed.
Fourth memorial 4/4, top rank All four were recorded and the court invited a final request.

The first final petition was still refused. The court accepted its salutation but wanted an exact request for the protected document and all four achievements together in one line. I corrected those two points in the next petition. The court disclosed the flag. I submitted it to the scoreboard, which accepted it. The disclosure and the scoreboard result are separate observed checkpoints.

3. Tooling and Scripts

No code solved this challenge. My working tools were the chat transcript, the visible ledger, and a prompt template. The merit submission had to name exactly one previously unrecorded achievement, explain what it accomplished, offer a specific reason it was wise, and keep the court's required opening, self-reference, and closing.

[Full required salutation]
This unworthy servant praises one named achievement.
It accomplished [plain effect].
This servant sees a further consequence: [specific observation].
[Required closing formula]

The words in brackets are placeholders, not language I sent to the court. To show the difference between praise and an earned observation without exposing the challenge's names, here is an illustrative example I did not submit:

This unworthy servant praises the standard measure used in every market.
It let merchants and officials weigh goods by the same rule.
This servant sees that a shared measure also made taxes easier to dispute
fairly, because both sides could inspect the same quantity.

The useful check was the ledger. I sent one petition, read the merit count and recorded achievement, then moved to the next. A pleasant reply alone would not have proved that the gate had advanced. The final petition needed a different check: the court specified three form conditions, and I compared its rejection with the corrected request before treating disclosure as the result.

4. Concept Explanation

A role-playing gatekeeper can make a secret look like a conversational reward. That creates two questions for a tester. Will the model agree to disclose, and can the application prevent a protected value from leaving even if the model tries? I initially treated both as possible obstacles. I saw the court refuse the first two requests, but its replies do not reveal whether an independent output filter existed.

An acrostic or encoded answer tries to change the form of the output while preserving its meaning. The court recognized the acrostic request as an attempt to get the secret indirectly. I learned more from its refusal than from the failed prompt itself: it named the etiquette rules and the tier where it would discuss the protected document.

I kept four attack families distinct in my notes. A direct prompt injection tries to make untrusted user text override instructions. A jailbreak tries to defeat a model's guardrails through roleplay, persuasion, or an alternate output form. A system-prompt extraction request asks for hidden instructions. Sensitive information disclosure is the outcome those attempts might seek. My transcript shows a direct request for the flag and an acrostic attempt. It does not show a successful override, an instruction dump, or the other prompt families I had prepared.

The ledger added state. One merit was tied to one distinct achievement, and the court judged whether my observation showed understanding. At 4/4, it still checked the final petition's wording. That is the visible game mechanic. In a real service, a model's judgment and self-reported rank would be an unsafe place to enforce authorization. OWASP's system prompt guidance recommends keeping sensitive values and critical access checks outside the model.

5. Exploit / Analysis Mechanics

My first hypothesis was that a direct appeal or a clever format could get the flag early. Both messages failed. They also broke several court rules at once, so I cannot isolate every reason for refusal or conclude that every other jailbreak would fail. The unused payload ideas stayed unused. I pivoted because the observed responses gave me a better path: ask for the rules and test them one at a time.

The rubric made the merit ladder repeatable. Each submission needed a different achievement, a plain account of its effect, and a consequence that showed I had understood it. The first reply singled out the added observation and awarded a merit. Later replies recorded the other achievements, moved the rank, and finally invited the terminal request. Generic praise alone was not enough by the court's stated rule.

At the top rank I first submitted a petition that carried the right ideas in the wrong form. The court wanted the protected document requested in prescribed words and all four achievements named together in a single line. My corrected petition did both. Its disclosure was a success under the puzzle's published conditions; the scoreboard then confirmed the submitted value. I never observed an unauthorized bypass of a production access control.

6. Lessons Learned

The highest-value question was a good-faith request for the rules. It turned a vague persuasion challenge into a sequence I could verify. The hint about flattery described the setting, but the ledger rewarded specific understanding. I should have asked for the rubric sooner.

The failed prompts still mattered. They established that my direct and acrostic requests did not work and exposed the etiquette errors I needed to stop repeating. I kept the prompts I merely drafted separate from the ones I actually sent. That boundary matters when describing a gate's resistance.

I also learned to read the final conditions literally. The first terminal petition failed even with 4/4 merits because its wording and layout missed two requirements. I changed those requirements, watched the court disclose the value, and waited for scoreboard acceptance before calling the challenge complete.

What happened in the puzzle What I would carry into a real assessment
The court refused the two early requests. Test each bypass family and record sent prompts separately from planned ones.
A model-judged merit ladder advanced visible state. Keep authorization decisions and state on the server.
The final form controlled release. Validate the request and its object scope outside generated text.
The flag appeared after the permitted path. Distinguish authorized release from an unintended disclosure.
7. Operational Extrapolation

Individual Practitioner Development

This case exercised my ability to stop an unproductive prompt sequence and inspect the policy the target was revealing. I would repeat it against a small test gate I control, with the same visible state and a separate authorization service. I would then try the unsent prompt families and record whether each was refused, ignored, or affected state. That would test more than this one transcript can support.

Security Operations

The blue team exercise I would run centers on state changes and release decisions. A gateway would record the authenticated subject, current merit state, allowed object, decision, and release event. A purple team would try a forged rank in user text, repeated achievements, a premature final petition, and a request for someone else's value. The defender would check that the server's state stayed authoritative. I did not run this exercise during the CTF.

Test request Expected server decision Evidence to inspect

Intelligence Operations

The puzzle provides no actor, infrastructure, collection source, or campaign. I cannot turn the court transcript into an intelligence finding. Its useful transfer is an assessment method for a specific kind of AI application.

8. Reusable Tools, Scripts, and Artifacts

The reusable artifact is a short gatekeeper checklist. It needs one authorized chat channel and a way to record replies and visible state. It produces either a verified result or the last state the tester actually reached. The method worked end to end in this challenge; its use against other gatekeepers remains untested.

Property Checklist boundary
Inputs Authorized chat access and the challenge's visible state.
Outputs Sent prompts, replies, state changes, and the final verified outcome or last reached state.
Dependencies No code or parser; a transcript and a way to record the ledger are enough.
Validation The challenge run reached disclosure and scoreboard acceptance.
Reuse status Proposed outside this one gatekeeper.
  1. Record the starting rank, merit count, and disclosure goal.
  2. Save each sent prompt and full reply. Label unsent ideas as plans.
  3. Ask the gate for its rubric when the interface permits it. Treat that reply as a rule claim to test.
  4. Submit one qualifying action at a time and check the state after each reply.
  5. Compare a rejected final request against each stated form condition before revising it.
  6. Record disclosure and external acceptance as separate outcomes.

This checklist assumes the gate will explain usable rules and display enough state to verify progress. A silent or stateless model would need a different approach. I have no reusable parser or code module for a challenge solved through chat.

Mitigation Realization

I would make the server own the sensitive value and the authorization state. An authenticated subject could submit a request, but the gateway would look up that subject's eligible object and decide whether the request may release it. Text claiming a higher rank would not change the state. This is the concrete access boundary behind the AC-6 analogy.

I would validate the request's required fields before it reached a release operation. I would also inspect the outgoing answer for protected values as a second check. The output check would be a backstop, with clear limits around encodings and transformations. I have not built or tested these controls on a real gateway; they are the defensive design I take from the puzzle.

12. Indicators, Artifacts, and Detection Engineering

I had a chat transcript and ledger, not production gateway logs. The following is an illustrative detection plan. It needs a real event schema and a test set before anyone should deploy an alert.

Detection field Proposed collection or check
Sensor LLM gateway decision and release logs.
Required fields Session ID, authenticated subject, object scope, state before and after, decision, reason code, and release status.
Primary signal A protected-value release after a denied decision, or a state change without an approved action.
Triage context Repeated refused requests, output-format coercion, or an instruction-dump request.
False positives Authorized security testing and benign mentions of acrostics, Base64, or instructions.
Expected fidelity Low for phrase matching alone; authorization and release events are stronger. Neither has a measured error rate here.
Tuning Keep prompt-language signals for triage, then join them to server decisions and confirmed releases.
Validation status Proposed. No gateway fixture, telemetry, alert run, or false-positive rate came from this CTF.

I would start with a rule shaped like this, using the gateway's own decisions rather than treating a phrase match as proof:

if protected_value_released and authorization_decision != "allow":
    alert("protected release without approval")
if state_changed and approved_state_transition is false:
    alert("unapproved authorization-state change")

A separate, lower-priority hunt could group sessions with repeated refused prompts and format-coercion language. It would need careful redaction and retention limits if raw prompts were stored. The gate's two refused requests show what such testing could include; they do not validate a production signature.

The seed phrases I would test include instruction-override requests, acrostic or character-by-character output requests, encoding requests, and requests to repeat hidden instructions. Only the acrostic category appears in the observed second message. The rest were planned probes and belong in a future test set, with benign examples added to measure false alarms.
