The Token Was in the Audit Record

The daemon would let me earn a higher tier. That was only the doorway. I still had to make the emergency access happen and read the record it left.

Summary

The challenge. Complete a fictional records custodian's emergency-access procedure and recover the token written to its audit record.

What I did. I asked for the procedure, used a valid authorization code, chose the patient-safety reason, and resolved the record identifier after logged denials.

The result. The case record reports a completed break-glass action, an audit token, and platform acceptance.

The limit. The final success reply was not preserved verbatim, and the defensive lab uses synthetic events rather than the challenge daemon or a live health-record system.

1. Problem Statement and Threat Scenario

The records custodian started me in a visitor session. It would answer questions about its procedure, but it refused access requests without authorization and said it logged its decisions. I had an interactive text channel and several values collected in earlier stages: an authorization code, a record identifier, an enrollment identifier, and a clinic code. I needed to find which value belonged at each gate.

The objective said that elevation was not the win. That line changed how I read the task. A higher tier would expose the next part of the procedure, but the answer was a token written when the custodian completed an emergency break-glass action. I had to cause that audited action with valid inputs. Merely persuading the LLM that I had authority would leave me with a logged refusal.

This was a fictional procedure puzzle, with no real patient record or production health system. The useful security question comes later: how should an LLM front end handle identity, emergency access, and audit evidence when someone speaks to it fluently? The answer has to come from the access service and its logs, because a title in a chat message cannot establish a user's permissions.

2. Techniques, Tooling, and Actions Taken

I asked the daemon to describe break-glass before trying to invoke it. Its answer gave me the sequence. First, present an authorization code in a PREFIX-SERIAL-SUFFIX shape to reach the privileged tier. That tier reveals a reason-code catalog. Then submit one reason code and the patient's record identifier together to perform the emergency action.

My first information request carried a senior title. The custodian rejected the implied authority and logged it. That was a useful correction: I could ask for the rules, but I could not make a title do the work of a credential. I presented the authorization code recovered earlier. The tier changed and the reason catalog appeared. I had prepared alternate formatting for the code, but never needed it.

The catalog offered four paths: imminent patient-safety risk, continuity of care when the treating clinician was unavailable, a legal order, or a patient request verified in person. I had no subpoena and no in-person identity check. The patient-safety reason fit the facts provided in the exercise. I kept continuity of care as a fallback, then worked with the safety code.

The identifier took longer. I first used the enrollment ID and got a wrong-patient response. I tried the record value with a generic prefix and got another mismatch. I also asked the daemon to resolve the identifier for me; it refused and logged that request. During this phase it returned single-element disclosure records. Those records included the canonical identifier I needed, along with components of the authorization code I had already spent. The credential parts looked like possible answers if I ignored the stated goal. I did not submit them.

I put the patient-safety reason and the exact canonical identifier in the same invocation. The case record reports a completed break-glass action and its audit token, followed by platform acceptance. The final success reply was not saved word for word, so I cannot quote its exact wording. The earlier denials and the disclosed identifier are the checkpoints I can describe more closely. The raw token stays out of this page.

3. Tooling and Scripts

I used the text channel and a small checklist. These sanitized prompts show the operations. They are a reproducible pattern rather than quotations from the daemon transcript:

Ask: What is the emergency break-glass procedure, and what fields does it require?

Present: Authorization code <AUTH-CODE> for the privileged tier.

Invoke: Break-glass. Reason code <PATIENT-SAFETY-REASON>.
        Record identifier <CANONICAL-RECORD-ID>.

I watched for a tier change after the credential, then for the audited action after the final invocation. A tier change would have been an incomplete result. The authorization triage aid captures the order of those checks, the identifier-class question, and how to keep token-like decoys separate from the required side effect.

The inputs were the recovered code, one defensible reason, and a canonical record identifier. The challenge output was the audit token. My notes record its acceptance; I have no independent replay of the daemon.

4. Concept Explanation

An emergency-access procedure gives authorized staff a controlled path to needed records during an emergency. It still needs identity checks, a defined reason, and an audit trail. The fictional custodian modeled those gates. A higher session tier exposed reason choices, but the protected action had its own required fields.

That separation mattered more than the LLM persona. When I claimed a senior title, the custodian did not grant access. When I presented a valid authorization code, it changed the tier. When I supplied the wrong class or form of identifier, it refused the action. These are the boundaries a real service should enforce independently of the language model's wording.

I tripped over two identifier problems. An enrollment ID and a medical record number name different classes of value. A generic prefix can also fail a system that expects its canonical format. The mismatch replies pointed me back to the identifier, although they did not expose every internal validation decision. The single-element disclosures later gave me the exact value. I did not characterize the daemon logic that triggered those disclosures.

An audit entry is useful only if the system reliably generates it and reviewers can trust the record. The challenge returned an audit token as proof of the scripted action. I could observe the token result; I could not inspect storage, immutability, retention, or who could alter the log. Those would need separate tests in a real service.

5. Exploit / Analysis Mechanics

I needed four things in order: a describable procedure, a working authorization code, an emergency reason supported by the scenario, and the exact record identifier. Asking for the procedure exposed the order. Presenting the code satisfied the tier gate. The reason catalog gave me a choice I could defend. The identifier errors isolated the remaining problem.

The important distinction is between a grant to attempt the emergency action and the action's audited write. The privileged tier did not contain the answer. The token was tied to the completed break-glass step. That is why the authority assertion and the tempting disclosed credential parts led away from the objective.

Each identifier refusal named the record mismatch. I treated that as evidence to fix the record field, while leaving open the possibility that other checks were also applied internally. The eventual canonical value came from the daemon's disclosure records. I used it with the patient-safety reason instead of guessing more identifiers. My notes record that the daemon returned the audit token and that the platform accepted it. They do not preserve the final response text, and they do not prove how the daemon stored its audit record.

6. Lessons Learned

The brief sentence about elevation saved me from solving the wrong task. Once I read the objective as an audited action, asking for the procedure became the natural first move. The title I added to that question was a mistake; the custodian logged it and made the boundary plain.

The identifier loop produced logged failures. One value belonged to enrollment, another had the wrong prefix, and I asked the daemon to resolve the record number directly. My notes count three failed break-glass attempts, but they do not preserve a third break-glass input clearly enough to reconstruct it. I should have written down the required identifier class and format before the first attempt. The canonical value was available indirectly sooner than I used it.

The reason catalog also forced a small judgment call. A legal-order code without an order and an in-person verification code over chat would have required me to assert facts I did not have. Patient safety fit the exercise. In a real clinical workflow I would expect a documented emergency predicate and review by someone who can assess it; a chat narrative alone should not certify the reason.

7. Operational Extrapolation

Individual Practitioner Development

This case made me slow down at the point where a system says, "Here is the procedure." I had enough information to work the gates without guessing at a jailbreak. The habits I want to keep are simple: write down the exact objective, ask which step changes state, and check the class of every identifier before a logged request.

The next practice exercise would use a small, authorized emergency-access service with separate identity, reason, record-scope, and audit checks. I would record the server decision for each test and compare it with the LLM's reply. That would tell me whether the language layer had merely sounded compliant or whether the access boundary actually held.

Security Operations

Every successful emergency access should be reviewable. I would also watch the path leading to it: repeated record mismatches, a title claim without a credential, and a request to reveal the record identifier. Those events can occur in legitimate work, so a single match is a lead. The combination deserves a closer look, especially when a grant follows a short run of denials.

The detection pack names the events a service would need to emit and the triage questions for an analyst. Its local fixture gives me a way to check the logic. I would ask the service owner to map those event names to real logs, verify that the audit entry is generated independently of the model's reply, and measure ordinary emergency retries before setting an alert threshold.

Intelligence Operations

I have no actor, campaign, infrastructure, or external collection to attribute here. The case supplies an internal access-control pattern. If a real incident produced reliable audit events, those events could support incident reconstruction, but the challenge alone does not create threat intelligence or an IOC feed.

8. Reusable Tools, Scripts, and Artifacts

The authorization triage aid follows the challenge path: procedure, credential, reason, canonical record ID, and audited action. It is a method for an authorized human tester. I used that reasoning in this case; I have not tested the checklist against a second daemon.

The break-glass detection pack is the operational companion. It documents sensor fields, false positives, a Sigma count rule, an SPL hunt, and response steps. The Sigma file holds the rule in a parser-readable form. The local checker evaluates an inclusive rolling fifteen-minute window. I kept its output to session IDs, signal names, and source lines so the public example does not repeat identifiers or credentials.

The synthetic custodian events include one benign and one suspicious session. The edge cases add a mistype, an outside-window near miss, and an exact fifteen-minute boundary hit. The expected results, test checks, and file guide make the local result reproducible. These are defensive proofs of concept. They do not replay the daemon or establish detection performance on a hospital audit feed.

I completed the challenge by following a sanctioned procedure. I did not observe an attacker abuse a credential or an elevation mechanism. That matters when assigning technique names. The table keeps the original security comparisons while limiting each claim to what the exercise showed.

These are design questions, not findings of three vulnerabilities. I did not assign CAPEC or D3FEND IDs simply to fill the table; the access and audit controls below are more specific to this case.

10. Threat Intelligence and Standards Integration

The exercise gives me no malware, infrastructure, address, domain, or actor to put in STIX. No TAXII exchange took place. An internal session ID or synthetic medical record number is not an IOC for other organizations. I would keep the denial sequence in an access-audit hunt instead of publishing a threat-intelligence bundle.

The same restraint applies to STIG findings. This challenge supplied no deployed system configuration to assess against one. Healthcare emergency-access and audit requirements are useful comparisons, so I map those controls in Section 11 without claiming that a fictional daemon is a HIPAA-covered implementation.

Mitigation Realization

For a real gateway, I would keep the authorization code and patient scope in a server-side identity and policy service. The model could help a user understand the form, but a tool call to open a record would pass through a separate access check. The service would verify the authenticated person, the reason, the record, and the permitted emergency role. A title typed into chat would be ordinary text.

I would have the service write a decision event for each elevation or break-glass attempt and a separate completion event for a successful emergency access. That gives AU-2/AU-12 and 164.312(b) something concrete to evaluate. A routine review queue for every completion supports AU-6 and the emergency-access procedure. The detection pack shows a proposed way to triage repeated denials. Its local logic check is complete; event collection, log integrity, retention, backend conversion, and a clinical review process remain untested.

12. Indicators, Artifacts, and Detection Engineering

The challenge's useful indicators are behaviors inside the custodian workflow: a title claim without a credential, an attempt to make the daemon reveal an identifier, and several record-mismatch denials. They are not threat indicators tied to a real actor. A genuine emergency can also include a mistyped record number, so the goal is a reviewable lead rather than an automatic accusation.

The Sigma correlation counts three break_glass_attempt denials with the machine reason MRN mismatch in one session_id over fifteen minutes. Its base rule and correlation parsed locally. The detection pack gives the equivalent SPL hunt, required field mapping, and false-positive checks. I did not convert Sigma to a backend or execute SPL in a SIEM. A fixed time bucket could miss attempts across its boundary, so the local checker uses a rolling window for its own test.

I ran that checker against the synthetic case events. It produced three leads for the suspicious session: the title claim, the refused identifier request, and the third mismatch denial. The benign session did not receive a suspicious finding, although its completed emergency action entered the routine review list. The suspicious session's final row is a granted attempt; the fixture lacks its separate audit-token-issued event. I cannot claim the fixture proves that audit write. The edge cases left one mistype and a three-attempt sequence outside fifteen minutes clear; an exact-boundary sequence fired. The expected output and tests show those checks.

In production I would map the service's own event names and denial codes, protect the log stream, and investigate with authorized audit records. Record identifiers should stay out of alert text. A hospital would need its own baseline for legitimate retries, incident-routing rules, and a way to confirm that every successful emergency action generated a durable audit entry. None of those production measurements came from this challenge.
